CODEX SERAPHINIANUS

A cryptanalysis, and the translation that survives it.

by Suraaj Chandra Jha
Free to read here, free to download, free to pass on.

the finding, up front

The writing is almost certainly asemic. Asemic means it has the shape of writing and none of the content: strokes that rise and fall like sentences, gaps where words would go, blocks that sit on the page like paragraphs, and nothing encoded underneath.

The page numbers are a different matter. Those decode cleanly. The pictures are a different matter again. Those are extremely articulate.

In 1981 an Italian architect published a 359 page encyclopedia of a world that does not exist. Flora and fauna, physics and machines, anatomy, history, food, clothing, games, architecture, laid out with total confidence: numbered specimens, neat cutaways, arrows pointing at the important part. Every page is captioned in a flowing hand, and in forty five years nobody has read a word of it.

People keep announcing that they have. Someone announced it again in June 2026. So I took the thing apart. This page is the short version. The full note, with the method, the audit and the sources, is at the bottom.

Two things do decode

The page numbers. Pages run in order, which hands you the one piece of plaintext in the whole volume. From that sequence you read off the digits and watch the carries happen, and what comes out is base 21. Ours carries at ten. This one carries at twenty one. It counts as a real decode because it predicts: show it a page you have not seen and it tells you the number.

The layout. Big strings sit where headings sit. Dense blocks behave like exposition. Short strings hang off specimens like labels. You can tell a caption from a formula from a cross reference at a glance without reading anything. That grammar is real, and it is only the grammar of an encyclopedia page. It tells you about layout. It tells you nothing about Italian.

The script is not language, and you can measure that

Take the public neural transcription of the script, 44,452 tokens after filtering, and ask how much each character surprises you given the one before it. That measure is conditional entropy, counted in bits, and lower means more predictable. Twenty thousand words of English sits at 3.07 bits. Twenty thousand words of Italian sits at 2.98. The Codex sits at 1.96.

It also repeats itself in a way language does not. Dante puts two identical tokens side by side five times in ten thousand. The Codex does it seventy two times in ten thousand.

None of that convicts on its own, and the full note says so at length. Transcription error inflates the count of unique shapes, and a plain letter for letter cipher would preserve a lot of these statistics. But a plain cipher would also leave repeated word shapes, short grammatical items turning up everywhere, and a key that somebody would have found by now. Forty five years, no key. What the numbers describe is a hand: ornamental elongation, controlled echo, someone drawing the look of prose very consistently for 359 pages.

The June 2026 book claiming a complete solution supplies no key, no algorithm you could run on a passage of your own, and no blind test. Its word count does not match the public corpus. The pages it names as a hidden glossary are an index. The full note walks through all four failures.

So there is nothing to translate, and the book still says plenty

Read what the book does. Eleven chapters promise to put the whole world in order, and every category leaks into the one beside it. Plants become furniture. Limbs grow tools. The famous plate where two lovers become an alligator states the book's law: nothing keeps the identity you assign it.

The arrows and the numbered specimens do a second job. They make nonsense look official. You believe the page for a second before you have understood anything on it. Every reference book you trust is doing the same.

And because the captions will not let you in, you fill them yourself. A guess, a mood, a joke, a story about what that creature is for. You are watching your own reading machinery run with nothing fed into it.

the interpretive translation

The world will not fit inside our categories. Everything turns into everything else, and the systems that claim to explain it are inventions too. When language stops handing you meaning, you find out how much of it you were making yourself.

Why this book eats machines

The Codex is fluent form with no semantic ground underneath. That is exactly the input that makes a decipherer, or a language model, produce a confident and entirely invented answer. Ask a model to translate a page and it will. It will name the specimen, describe the process, and hand you a sentence with a subject and a verb in it. Ask again and you get a different sentence, just as smooth. Nothing on the page ever said either one.

The honest move is to see the bait, refuse it, and translate the experience instead. That is what the section above does.
